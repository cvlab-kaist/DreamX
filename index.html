Overview

One action space for every embodiment

The video prior handles appearance; a compact latent carries the motion. Encode once, then reuse the action for video transfer, action transfer, or policy learning.

DreamX learns a shared action interface from human egocentric and robot videos. Latent actions read out of a pretrained video diffusion model capture motion across changes in embodiment and scene. The same representation transfers motion to a new video, turns human motion into robot actions with no paired training data, and drives a compact latent policy.

Method

How DreamX works

DreamX is built on Cosmos-Predict2.5 (2B). The same pretrained video diffusion backbone, adapted with LoRA, plays two roles: an encoder that reads motion out and a decoder that checks the motion explains the future.

1

Read out motion

Learnable query tokens are appended to the video tokens and processed by the backbone’s spatiotemporal attention. Four queries per latent frame summarize its dynamics, about 300× fewer tokens than the frame itself.

2

Reconstruct the future

A decoder initialized from the same backbone regenerates the future frames from only the first frame and the latent actions. The first frame already supplies appearance, so the latent actions are pushed to carry the motion.

3

Reuse anywhere

Swap in a new first frame for video transfer, or a robot state for action transfer. For control, a compact latent policy predicts compressed action codes instead of future video.

Latent action pretraining. Queries read the backbone’s features into latent actions (yellow). The decoder denoises future video, and robot actions when labels exist, from only the first frame and those latent actions.

Pretraining data

2.19k hours of human and robot video

DreamX is pretrained on six datasets: egocentric human demonstrations plus four robot embodiments. Human videos carry no action labels; they train the latent actions through video reconstruction alone.

    Motion transfer · video

    Video transfer

    The frozen encoder turns a source video into latent actions; the frozen decoder replays them from a new first frame. The source motion plays out in the target scene and embodiment, with no paired data and no extra training.

    Video transfer. Source latent actions + a target first frame → a new video of the source motion in the target scene and embodiment.

    Any-to-any video transfer

    Across scenes, across robots, from humans to robots, and beyond manipulation.

    Zero-shot human → robot video transfer

    On the HumanAndRobot benchmark, DreamX follows the human’s motion while keeping the robot and the scene intact. AdaWorld degrades visually, and VideoWorld2 sometimes leaks the human hand into the robot video.

    Action transfer

    The same latent actions can be decoded into robot actions instead of pixels. From a human video’s latent actions and only the target robot’s initial state, the decoder’s action branch outputs executable low-level actions, with no paired human–robot data.

    Analysis

    Where do latent actions look?

    Query-to-video attention during encoding. The latent-action queries focus on the hands and grippers in contact with objects, for both humans and robots. They read out the action-relevant regions instead of the background.

    Control

    A compact latent policy

    Instead of generating future video, the DreamX policy predicts compact latent-action codes. A variational bottleneck shrinks each 2,048-d latent token to a 32-d code. The policy denoises those codes from the current observation and instruction, and a separate DiT head turns them into robot actions. Human videos supervise the codes; robot data supervises the actions.

    Left: the bottleneck compresses frozen latent actions into compact codes and reconstructs them. Right: the policy denoises compact codes (yellow) from text and observation, while the Action DiT reads the backbone’s latent-action features and predicts low-level actions (blue).

    Quantitative results

    State of the art on LARYBench

    LARYBench asks whether a latent action keeps enough fine-grained motion to regress end-effector trajectories, and enough semantics to classify motions. DreamX leads every benchmark, and its 32-channel compressed codes (FAE-32) still beat all baselines.
